Teeth Whitening: What Works, What Wastes Money, What Harms

Dr. Vidya Kadiyala 18 May 2026 6 min read

Charcoal, baking soda, lemon, activated whitening toothpaste, LED kits — sorted into effective, useless and actively damaging.

There are two separate things people mean by whiter teeth: removing stain that sits on the surface, and lightening the actual colour of the tooth underneath. Most home remedies attempt the first and claim the second.

What works

In-clinic whitening uses a controlled concentration of hydrogen or carbamide peroxide, applied with the gums isolated and protected. It lightens the intrinsic shade of the tooth and typically lifts several shades in a single 60 to 90 minute session.

Professionally supervised take-home kits use custom trays and a lower-strength gel worn for a set period each day. Slower, but the results are comparable and easier to top up later.

Professional scaling and polishing removes tartar and surface stain from tea, coffee and tobacco. It does not change the underlying shade, but for many people it reveals a tooth colour they had forgotten they had — which is why we always clean before whitening.

What wastes money

Whitening toothpastes work by mild abrasion and can remove some surface stain. They cannot change intrinsic tooth colour, whatever the packaging implies.

LED lights sold with home kits add very little. Most of the effect comes from the gel; the light is largely theatre.

Oil pulling has no evidence of whitening effect. It is harmless, but it is not doing what the videos claim.

What actively harms

  • Charcoal powders. Highly abrasive. They scrub away enamel, and the dentine exposed underneath is yellower — so teeth look darker over time, not lighter.
  • Lemon juice and vinegar. Acidic enough to dissolve enamel directly. Enamel does not grow back.
  • Baking soda used daily. Fine occasionally, abrasive as a daily habit.
  • Unregulated online whitening gels. Concentrations are often far above the legal limit for home use, with no gum protection — chemical burns and severe sensitivity are common results.

What whitening cannot fix

Peroxide only lightens natural tooth structure. It has no effect on crowns, veneers, bridges or composite fillings. If you whiten teeth that sit alongside existing restorations, those restorations will suddenly look darker and may need replacing to match — plan the sequence with your dentist first.

Deep intrinsic staining from tetracycline or fluorosis also responds poorly to whitening alone. Veneers or bonding are usually the realistic route there.

Making it last

Expect one to two years from a professional whitening, depending on habits. Coffee, tea, red wine and tobacco shorten it considerably. Rinsing with water after staining drinks, and a periodic top-up with a take-home kit, keeps the result stable.
