Bleeding Gums Are Not Normal — Here's What They Mean

Dr. Sujatha Reddy 30 April 2026 6 min read

Most adults in India have some degree of gum disease and do not know it. The earliest stage is completely reversible; the next one is not.

If your hands bled every time you washed them, you would see a doctor. Gums get a pass because the bleeding is mild, painless and easy to rationalise as brushing too hard. It almost never is.

Gingivitis: reversible

The first stage is gingivitis — inflammation of the gum caused by plaque sitting at the gum line. The gums look puffy and red rather than firm and pale pink, and they bleed when brushed or flossed.

At this stage nothing has been permanently lost. A professional cleaning to remove the hardened deposits, followed by consistent brushing and interdental cleaning, resolves it completely within a couple of weeks.

Periodontitis: not reversible

Left untreated, the inflammation spreads below the gum line and starts destroying the bone that holds the tooth in place. Pockets form between tooth and gum, filling with bacteria that a toothbrush cannot reach.

Bone that has been lost does not grow back on its own. Treatment at this stage aims to halt the progression, not reverse it — which is exactly why the bleeding stage matters so much.

Signs beyond bleeding

  • Persistent bad breath that returns within hours of brushing.
  • Gums that have receded, making teeth look longer.
  • A bad taste, or sensitivity along the gum line.
  • Teeth that feel slightly loose, or a bite that feels different.
  • Gaps opening up between teeth that were previously tight.

Why it is not just about teeth

Periodontal disease has well-documented associations with diabetes, cardiovascular disease and adverse pregnancy outcomes. The relationship with diabetes runs both ways: poorly controlled blood sugar worsens gum disease, and active gum disease makes blood sugar harder to control.

What actually prevents it

Brushing twice daily for two minutes with a soft brush, angled into the gum line. Cleaning between the teeth daily with floss or an interdental brush — this is the step most people skip, and it is where gum disease starts. A professional cleaning every six months, or every three to four months if you are already at risk.

One correction worth making: scaling does not loosen teeth. It removes the tartar that was splinting them while quietly destroying the bone. Teeth can feel slightly mobile for a few days afterwards as the inflamed tissue heals — that is recovery, not damage.
