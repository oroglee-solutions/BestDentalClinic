Your Child's First Dental Visit: A Parent's Guide

Dr. Sujatha Reddy 5 June 2026 5 min read

When to come, what actually happens, and the three things parents say that make children more afraid, not less.

The goal of a first dental visit is not treatment. It is to make sure the child leaves thinking the clinic is a boring, harmless place. Everything else follows from that.

When to come

By the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing — whichever comes first. That feels early to most parents, but early visits are almost entirely preventive: a quick look, a risk assessment, and guidance on brushing and diet.

What happens at the appointment

For a toddler, the examination often happens knee-to-knee with the parent, not in the chair. For an older child we start with a ride in the chair, a count of the teeth, and a look with a mirror. Nothing sharp, nothing loud, unless the child is comfortable.

We check for early decay, look at how the jaws are developing, and ask about habits — thumb sucking, mouth breathing, bottle at bedtime. Then we talk to the parent about brushing technique and snacking, and set a recall date.

Three things not to say

  1. 1"It won't hurt." The child hears the word hurt and nothing else. Say the dentist will count your teeth instead.
  2. 2"Be brave." This tells a child there is something to be brave about. Keep it matter-of-fact.
  3. 3"If you're good, we'll get ice cream." A reward framed around an ordeal confirms it was an ordeal — and undermines the diet conversation we just had.

The most useful thing a parent can bring is a calm, slightly bored attitude. Children read the adult in the room before they read the dentist.

Preventing cavities at home

Brush twice daily with an age-appropriate fluoride toothpaste, and supervise until around age eight — younger children do not have the manual control to clean the back teeth properly. A useful rule: if they cannot tie their own shoelaces, they cannot brush unsupervised.

On diet, frequency matters more than quantity. A chocolate eaten in one sitting after a meal is far less damaging than the same chocolate nibbled across an afternoon, because each exposure restarts the acid attack.

Do milk teeth matter?

Yes. Decayed milk teeth cause genuine pain and infection, and losing one early lets neighbouring teeth drift into the gap — crowding the permanent tooth that follows. Treating them protects the adult teeth underneath.
